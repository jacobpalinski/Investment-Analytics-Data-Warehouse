# Nasdaq Market Analytics Dashboard

## Overview
Quantitative trading and investment decisions rely on analysing signals across multiple time horizons, from real-time market information to slower-moving fundamental, macroeconomic, and sentiment drivers. In practice, these signals are often distributed across different tools and update frequencies, making it difficult to contextualise short-term price behaviour within broader market and company-level trends. This disconnect can lead to overreacting to intraday noise, delayed recognition of shifts in market behaviour such as volatility, liquidity, or trend dynamics, or inefficient validation of trading signals against underlying fundamentals.

The dashboard (and data warehouse) developed as part of this project addresses this challenge by combining up-to-date market, sentiment, macroeconomic, and company analytics with a dedicated near-real-time monitoring layer for individual stocks. It allows users to analyse market-wide return and volume patterns, identify top movers, and evaluate sentiment and macroeconomic trends, while simultaneously monitoring intraday price action, liquidity, and volatility for selected securities in near real time. By separating higher-level analysis from intraday monitoring, yet housing both within a single interface, the dashboard supports more structured decision-making, improved signal validation, and better alignment between short-term trading behaviour and longer-term market drivers.

Full complete documentation can be found here: https://jacobs-organization-35.gitbook.io/nasdaq-market-analytics-dashboard

## Challenges
### Designing Data Architecture
I was required to deal with the challenges of designing the data architecture, which involved making key decisions around the frequency of updates and the granularity of data across different layers of the system. A major challenge was determining how to balance near-real-time updates for stock aggregates data with slower, batch-processed datasets like company financials, sentiment analysis, and macroeconomic indicators. These datasets varied significantly in their refresh rates and granularity, which made it difficult to design a unified model that remained both accurate and performant. For example, while stock aggregate data was updated in near real-time to support timely market analysis, financial and economic datasets were processed on less frequent schedules because their underlying source data changed significantly less often. In addition, I had to carefully consider the tradeoffs between cost and maintainability, as more frequent updates and higher-granularity data would increase computational overhead and storage requirements, while overly simplified models risked reducing analytical value. This required iterative refinement of the architecture to ensure that the system remained scalable, cost-efficient, and capable of supporting both real-time and historical analysis use cases.

### Dimensional Modelling
This was a complicated process as I had to integrate from different sources with different granularities. In particular, I was required to integrate near real-time Nasdaq stock aggregations (minute level), financial data (FY quarter level), sentiment data (day level), economic indicators data (day level) and company-specific information such as CIKs, ticker symbols and industry. In the initial design of the dimensional model, it was the intention to have a single fact table with near-real-time stock aggregations and dimensions for the corresponding financial, sentiment, economic and company-specific data. Having stock aggregations as the fact table and corresponding dimensions, however, would require using bridging tables to match the data in the dimensions to match the granularity of the fact table, increasing ETL processing complexity and making queries for the dashboard more computationally expensive, as more joins would be required. Secondly, each dataset contains data elements that are measurable and change over time and would not be suited as dimensions supporting a single fact table. As such, separate fact tables were created for stock aggregates, company financials, sentiment and economic indicators. This approach reduced the need for complex bridging logic, simplified analytical queries, and better reflected the independent business processes represented by each dataset. Given the different granularities, a decision was also made to have all times recorded in UTC for consistency at the event time. 

### Implementing Infrastructure as Code (IAC)
This proved to be difficult as it required coordinating infrastructure provisioning, container deployment, workflow orchestration, data ingestion services, and integration of AWS, Airflow, Snowflake, Kafka, Docker and Metabase. As such, there were numerous instances of debugging deployment failures caused by small configuration mistakes as well as failures with inconclusive error messages. Additionally, there was difficulty surrounding environment configuration, secret management and ensuring deployments behaved consistently across local and cloud environments. This high degree of operational complexity was more than I anticipated; however, it was a great learning opportunity to better understand the complexity of creating and managing production systems.

## Learnings
As part of automating the deployment process through CI/CD and infrastructure as code (IaC), I learned the importance of maintaining infrastructure consistency to support reliable deployments, secure configurations, and stable production environments. Although implementing these practices required a significant upfront investment of time, the process provided valuable insight into how cloud infrastructure is provisioned, managed, and maintained within AWS.

Working with both real-time and batch datasets that varied in granularity and update frequency highlighted the challenges of designing effective data architectures. I learned that architectural decisions require careful tradeoffs between timeliness, accuracy, scalability, cost, and maintainability. The project also reinforced that there is rarely a single optimal solution; instead, design choices must be guided by business requirements, data characteristics, and operational constraints.

Perhaps the most important lesson from this project was that successful data engineering extends beyond moving and transforming data. Building robust data platforms requires balancing technical and operational considerations while designing systems that can evolve alongside changing business needs and growing data volumes.
